The Dates of America's 250th

It isn't one day. It's a window that runs from 2025 to 2033 — and here is every date in it.

Almost everyone plans for one date. July 4, 2026 is the centerpiece, and it has already passed. The semiquincentennial is not over — it runs from the 250th of Lexington and Concord in April 2025 to the 250th of the Treaty of Paris in September 2033, and there is a 250th worth marking in almost every year between now and then.

The five dates that carry the story

July 4, 2026 — the Declaration

On July 2, 1776, the Second Continental Congress voted to separate from Great Britain. On July 4 it approved the final wording and ordered it printed. July 4 marks the day the text was adopted — the argument, not the vote and not the signing. That is why John Adams thought the country would celebrate the second of July. Sources: National Archives, "Declaration of Independence"; Library of Congress, Journals of the Continental Congress, 1774–1789, vol. 5.

December 26, 2026 and January 3, 2027 — Trenton and Princeton

Washington crossed the Delaware on the night of December 25–26, 1776, and took the Hessian garrison at Trenton in a morning action. Nine days later he slipped around the British force sent to trap him and won again at Princeton. These two weeks are the reason there was still an army in 1777. Sources: National Park Service, Washington Crossing and Princeton Battlefield; Library of Congress, George Washington Papers.

October 17, 2027 — Saratoga

British General John Burgoyne surrendered his army near Saratoga, New York, on October 17, 1777. This is the hinge: the loss of an entire British army persuaded France the Americans could win, and the formal alliance followed in February 1778. Sources: National Park Service, Saratoga National Historical Park; Yale Avalon Project, Treaty of Alliance with France, February 6, 1778.

October 19, 2031 — Yorktown

After a siege by combined American and French forces, with a French fleet holding the Chesapeake, Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown on October 19, 1781. This is the end of major fighting in North America — not the end of the war. Sources: National Park Service, Yorktown Battlefield; Library of Congress, Articles of Capitulation, October 19, 1781.

September 3, 2033 — the Treaty of Paris

American and British commissioners signed the definitive treaty on September 3, 1783. Britain recognized the United States as free and independent; the Confederation Congress ratified on January 14, 1784. Seven years and two months separate independence declared from independence acknowledged. Sources: National Archives, Milestone Documents, Treaty of Paris (1783); Yale Avalon Project, "Treaty of Paris, 1783."

Three things people get wrong

  • "The Declaration was signed on July 4." It was adopted on July 4, 1776. Most signatures went on the engrossed parchment starting August 2.
  • "Yorktown ended the war." Yorktown ended the major fighting in October 1781. The war ended legally with the Treaty of Paris, September 3, 1783.
  • "America 250 is a one-day event." The anniversaries run through 2033.

So what does the milestone actually mean?

Two hundred and fifty years is not a round number chosen for a party. It is the distance between an argument written by men who expected to be hanged for it and a country that is still arguing over what that document requires. The window from 1775 to 1783 is the reminder that the founding was not a day of good news — it was eight years of it being unclear whether any of it would hold. Marking the whole window, rather than one afternoon, is the difference between a celebration and a memory.

How this sheet is kept honest
