Part 1 — Your airline refund rights (federal, all U.S. flights)

The Department of Transportation's automatic-refund protections — finalized in its airline-refunds rulemaking (see the Federal Register rule) and summarized with effective dates by U.S. PIRG — phase in through 2025–2026. Here's what applies this summer.

When you are owed an AUTOMATIC cash refund

You don't have to ask twice, accept a voucher, or fill out a special form. The airline must refund the ticket price to your original payment method when:

  1. Your flight is canceled and you don't accept rebooking. Any reason, any airline, any fare class — including basic economy.
  2. Your flight is "significantly changed" and you decline the alternative. The federal definition of a significant change:
    • Departure or arrival moved 3+ hours domestic / 6+ hours international
    • Departure or arrival airport changed
    • More connections added than you booked
    • Downgrade to a lower class of service
    • A change to a connecting airport or aircraft that is less accessible for a passenger with a disability
  3. Your checked bag is delayed and not delivered within 12 hours of a domestic flight's gate arrival (15–30 hours international, by flight length) — you're owed a refund of the bag fee once you file a mishandled-bag report.
  4. You paid for an extra service you didn't get — Wi-Fi that never worked, a seat assignment not honored, priority boarding not provided. Refund of that fee.

The fine print that matters

  • Refund = cash / original payment, not miles or vouchers, unless you knowingly choose otherwise.
  • Timing: 7 business days for credit-card purchases, 20 calendar days for other payment methods.
  • You used part of the trip? You're owed the unused portion.
  • Nonrefundable ticket, you just changed plans? Not covered. These rights trigger on the airline's cancellation/change, not yours.

How to demand it (script)

"My flight [number] on [date] was canceled / significantly changed under DOT's refund rule. I decline rebooking and request an automatic refund to my original payment method within 7 business days as required. Please confirm in writing."

If the airline stalls: file a complaint at secure.dot.gov/air-travel-complaint — DOT complaints get airline-executive-level attention, and the rule has real enforcement teeth.

Part 2 — Subscription cancellation ("click-to-cancel")

The federal picture: back from the dead

The FTC's original "click-to-cancel" (Negative Option) rule was struck down on procedural grounds in 2025 — but the FTC revived click-to-cancel rulemaking in 2026, as reported by Jones Day's analysis. Until a new federal rule is final, your strongest rights are state and city law — and the FTC can still sue companies for deceptive subscription practices under existing law (ROSCA and the FTC Act) even without the rule.

What the "easy cancellation" principle means

  • Cancel the way you signed up. Signed up online? You must be able to cancel online — no mandatory phone call, no chat-agent gauntlet.
  • Clear disclosure before you're charged — price, renewal frequency, and how to cancel.
  • Consent for the subscription itself, separate from the rest of the transaction.
  • Renewal reminders for longer-term plans (in many state laws).

The state/city map (mid-2026 highlights)

  • California — the strongest state law: online cancellation required, annual renewal reminders, affirmative consent at renewal for auto-renewals (updated law in effect since July 2025).
  • New York City — the city's own click-to-cancel rule takes effect October 1, 2026: covered businesses must offer a cancellation mechanism as easy as sign-up. Mark the date; it covers a huge share of national subscription commerce.
  • New York State, Illinois, Oregon, Colorado, Virginia, and others — auto-renewal statutes requiring clear disclosure and simple cancellation; details vary, but the sign-up-parity principle is spreading.
  • Everywhere — unauthorized or undisclosed renewal charges can be disputed with your card issuer as billing errors.

How to actually cancel a hostile subscription (escalation ladder)

  1. In-app/online first — screenshot the confirmation (or the absence of any cancel option).
  2. Email the company stating you cancel effective immediately; email creates a timestamp.
  3. Card dispute — if charged after cancellation, dispute as an unauthorized charge; include your screenshots.
  4. Complain — FTC: reportfraud.ftc.gov; your state attorney general; in NYC after Oct 1, 2026, the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection.
  5. Virtual card / freeze — many banks let you kill the specific card number a subscription bills.

Cancellation script

"I am canceling my subscription effective today, [date]. Per [state] auto-renewal law, cancellation must be available by the same method I used to enroll. Confirm cancellation in writing and cease all future charges. Any charge after this notice will be disputed as unauthorized."

Quick reference

AIRLINE: Canceled or 3hr+/6hr+ change → automatic cash refund, 7 business days. Bag 12hr late → bag fee back. Paid extra you didn't get → fee back.
SUBSCRIPTION: Cancel how you signed up. Screenshot everything. Charged anyway → card dispute. NYC rule live Oct 1, 2026.
COMPLAINT LINES: DOT air travel: secure.dot.gov/air-travel-complaint · FTC: reportfraud.ftc.gov · Your state AG's consumer division.
This is general news and education, not legal advice. Rules phase in on different dates, agency rules are subject to litigation, and state laws vary — verify current requirements with DOT, the FTC, and your state before relying on them, and consult a licensed attorney for your specific situation.

Questions people actually ask

Consumer-rights questions, answered with the citation

Short answers, each sourced to the statute or regulation it comes from and verified against that text on August 7, 2026. This is general news and education, not legal advice; consult a licensed attorney for your situation.

What Qualifies as Debt Collector Harassment Under the Law?

15 U.S.C. § 1692d prohibits threats of violence, obscene or abusive language, publishing lists of consumers who allegedly refuse to pay, and calling repeatedly with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass. Regulation F adds a countable presumption at 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)(i). Lying about the debt is separately barred by § 1692e, and collecting fees not authorized by the agreement or by law by § 1692f.

What Is the 7-in-7 Rule for Debt Collectors?

It is the call-frequency presumption at 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)(i): more than seven calls within seven consecutive days about a particular debt — or a call within seven days of a conversation about that debt — is presumed to violate the harassment prohibition. The count is per debt, not per person. The full rule, with its edges.

Can I Tell a Debt Collector to Stop Calling?

Yes, in writing. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c), once you notify a collector in writing that you refuse to pay or want contact stopped, it must stop — except to tell you collection efforts are ending or to state that it may or will invoke a specific remedy. Know the trade-off: stopping contact does not cancel the debt or stop a lawsuit.

How Long Does a Landlord Have to Return My Security Deposit?

Your state sets it. In five verified states: California 21 days with an itemized statement (Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5); New York 14 days (N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108); Florida 15 days to return or 30 days to give written notice of a claim (Fla. Stat. § 83.49); Texas 30 days after surrender (Tex. Prop. Code § 92.103); Illinois 30 days to itemize and 45 for the balance (765 ILCS 710/1). The verified-states table.

What Deductions Can a Landlord Make From My Security Deposit?

The categories are set by statute, not by the lease. California's list at Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5(b) is representative: unpaid rent, repair of damages caused by the tenant exceeding ordinary wear and tear, and cleaning to return the unit to the level of cleanliness it had at the inception of the tenancy. Ordinary deterioration sits outside those categories.

What Is the Penalty if a Landlord Wrongfully Keeps My Deposit?

In Texas, bad-faith retention makes a landlord liable for $100 plus three times the wrongfully withheld amount plus attorney's fees (Tex. Prop. Code § 92.109). In Illinois, bad-faith refusal costs twice the deposit plus court costs and reasonable attorney's fees (765 ILCS 710/1(c)). In New York, missing the 14-day deadline forfeits the right to retain any part of the deposit (N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108).

Five verified states only — read your own state's section before relying on any table. General news and education, not legal advice.
