Pink noise vs brown noise vs white noise: the complete comparison

Noise "colours" are not marketing names — each one describes how the sound's energy is distributed across frequency, and that distribution is the whole difference between a track that disappears behind your thoughts and one that sits in the front of your head like static. This is the practical version: what each colour sounds like, what it covers well, and which ones we actually sell. Dusk's catalogue is deep brown, pink, fan hum and ocean swell. We do not sell white or grey noise, and we describe them here anyway because you cannot choose between four things by hearing only two of them.

The one rule that explains all of it

Every octave you go up covers twice as much frequency range as the one below. So a noise with equal power per hertz — white noise — ends up with half its total energy in its top octave alone, and your ear is at its most sensitive right about there. That single fact is why white noise sounds bright and effortful, why pink sounds balanced, and why brown sounds like weather in the distance. The colours are just different answers to how steeply the energy should fall as pitch rises.

The four colours, plainly

White noise — flat per hertz, bright to the ear

Equal power at every frequency. It is the reference point everyone starts from and the one most people bounce off: a hard, hissing sound, closest to an untuned FM radio or an extractor fan at close range. It is genuinely the strongest masker of high-pitched intrusions — a whining fridge, a shrill alarm two floors down, tinnitus-adjacent tones — because it puts the most energy exactly where those sounds live. It is also the colour that people describe as harsh, fatiguing, or "static in my skull". Dusk does not sell white noise.

Pink noise — equal energy per octave, the balanced middle

Pink noise falls about 3 dB per octave, which works out to equal energy in every octave rather than every hertz. That is the balance most natural continuous sound already has: steady rain, wind through trees, distant traffic. It is audibly softer than white without going dull, and it is the sensible starting point if you have no idea which colour you want. Our master is Pink Noise — 12 minutes: 720.0 seconds measured, and deliberately the quietest average level in the catalogue at a mean −20.8 dBFS, because masking works best when it is only just loud enough.

Brown noise — 6 dB per octave, the deep one

Twice pink's slope, so the top end is dramatically reduced and the low end dominates. This is the sound people reach for when white noise has already failed them: a low, weather-like roar with almost no hiss in it. It covers low and mid-range intrusions best — a television through a wall, footsteps upstairs, the low mumble of a conversation — and it is much easier to leave running for hours because there is very little in it for the ear to fatigue against. Our master is Deep Brown — 20 minutes: 1,200.0 seconds measured, rolled off above 700 Hz, mean −18.6 dBFS.

Grey noise — white, reshaped to how ears actually hear

Grey noise takes white noise and bends it to an equal-loudness contour, boosting the frequencies your ear is less sensitive to and cutting the ones it favours, so the result is meant to sound equally loud everywhere rather than measure equally loud everywhere. In practice that means a scooped-out midrange. Some people find it the most neutral of all four; plenty of others cannot hear the point of it. It is a preference, not an upgrade. Dusk does not sell grey noise.

The two textures that are not colours at all

Fan hum and ocean swell are shaped noise rather than pure colours, and for sleep they often beat the textbook options.

  • Fan Hum — 15 minutes (900.0 seconds, mean −18.5 dBFS) is brown noise rolled off above 380 Hz: almost no hiss at all, just the dull air-moving sound of a fan two rooms away. It is the least intrusive thing we make at low level, which matters if someone else is asleep in the room.
  • Ocean Swell — 18 minutes (1,080.0 seconds, mean −18.8 dBFS) is low brown noise under a very slow rise and fall — about 0.12 Hz, roughly one wave every eight seconds — rolled off above 650 Hz. No gulls, no music. It is the option for people who find flat noise boring, at the cost of a little masking consistency, because anything that moves is slightly easier to notice.

If white noise has already failed you

The most common report we see is some version of "white noise sounds too harsh" or "white noise keeps me awake". The instinct is to turn it down, which usually does not help, because the problem is the balance rather than the level — quieter glare is still glare. Moving down a colour is the fix that works: white to pink if you still need some brightness for high-pitched intrusions, white or pink to brown if you want the sound to sit underneath your hearing rather than on top of it, and brown to fan hum if even brown has more air in it than you want. Anyone sensitive to specific sounds tends to land at the brown-to-fan-hum end, where there is simply less material for the ear to pick at.

How to choose in one night, without buying anything. Play any free white noise, any free brown, and any free pink at a level just above the noise you are trying to cover. Do not compare them for thirty seconds each — compare them for twenty minutes each, because harshness is a fatigue effect and it does not show up straight away. Whichever one you stopped noticing is your colour. Then buy that one, or don't.

Steady noise or a nature texture?

Steady noise masks more reliably because it never varies: the floor stays exactly where you set it, so an intrusive sound has nothing to poke through. A texture that moves — ocean swell, rainfall, wind — is easier to enjoy and easier to notice, and if a door slams during the quiet part of a swell you will hear it. Choose steady when there is a specific noise you are fighting. Choose a texture when the flat wall of sound is itself the thing keeping you awake. Neither is the sophisticated answer; they solve different problems.

What we will not tell you

None of this is a treatment for anything. Masking is a physical effect on the sound in your room — it narrows the gap between the background level and a sudden noise so the sudden noise stands out less. That is the whole mechanism, and it says nothing about what happens in your head afterwards. Some people sleep better with steady noise, some sleep worse with it, and a systematic review of continuous overnight sound is a good deal less enthusiastic than the marketing around it. If your sleep problem is persistent, that is a conversation for a clinician rather than a speaker. We make no claims about infant sleep at all — ask a paediatrician.
