How to Make a Meme Without a Copyright Strike

"It's fair use" is the most confidently wrong sentence on the internet. Fair use is real, and it does protect a lot of meme culture — but it's a legal defense you'd raise after being sued or taken down, decided case-by-case by a judge weighing specific factors, not a blanket permission slip that makes copyright not apply. Understanding the actual shape of it is the difference between a meme that's genuinely low-risk and one that just hasn't been noticed yet.

This matters more the moment you go from posting a meme for laughs to putting it on a shirt, a printable, or anything with your name and a price tag on it — which is exactly the point where "everyone does this" stops being a legal argument.

1. Fair use is a defense, not a license

In the U.S., fair use (17 U.S.C. § 107) is an exception a court can find applies to a specific use of copyrighted material — it is not something you get to declare in advance and be automatically covered by. Nobody "owns" a fair-use meme format the way they own a piece of original art; they're making a bet that if challenged, a court would side with them. Most memes never get challenged, which is why the bet usually pays off in practice — but "usually doesn't get challenged" and "is legal" are different claims, and only one of them is true by default.

2. The four fair-use factors, briefly

Courts weigh four things, none of them decisive alone:

  • Purpose and character of the use. Commentary, criticism, and parody weigh in your favor; straight commercial reuse with no transformation weighs against you. A meme that comments on or recontextualizes the original image (the classic "expectation vs. reality" format) is on stronger ground than one that just reposts a photo with a caption slapped on.
  • Nature of the copyrighted work. Using a factual news photo weighs slightly better than using a highly creative, fictional work (a movie still, a piece of concept art).
  • Amount used. A single frame from a film is different from reposting the whole scene; less is generally safer, though this factor alone won't save an otherwise non-transformative use.
  • Effect on the market. If your meme could plausibly substitute for the original or hurt its market (using a photographer's entire portfolio image as your product's main art, say), that weighs heavily against fair use — this is often the deciding factor in practice.

None of these factors makes a meme "definitely fine." They make it more or less defensible if it's ever challenged — which is a different, more honest way to think about risk than "is this fair use, yes or no."

3. Separate "sharing a meme" from "selling a meme"

This is the distinction that actually matters for a merch or content business. Reposting a meme format in a group chat is extremely low-enforcement-risk in practice — platforms and rights holders rarely chase individual reposts. Selling a shirt, mug, or printable built on someone else's copyrighted photo, character, or footage is a different category entirely: it's a commercial use, factor one above weighs against you by default, and it's the exact kind of use rights holders and platforms (Etsy, Printify, Redbubble) actively police, because it's their own legal exposure too. A meme that's fine to post is not automatically fine to print and sell — treat those as two separate risk calculations, not one.

4. Use the safest asset categories

The lowest-risk path to a sellable meme isn't threading the fair-use needle at all — it's not needing to:

  • Original photography or AI-generated imagery you made or generated yourself, where you hold the rights outright.
  • True public-domain material — work whose copyright has expired (pre-1929 U.S. works, government works) — verified as public domain, not just "old" or "looks free."
  • Genuinely original text-based formats — the "what they said vs. what they meant" style, an archetype card, a decoder joke — where the humor lives entirely in your own writing and layout, with no third-party image at all. This is the format with zero copyright exposure, because there's nothing borrowed to have a claim on.

The third option is worth defaulting to for anything you plan to sell: it's not a fair-use bet at all, it's just yours.

5. Watch trademark and right of publicity too

Copyright isn't the only risk. A meme using a company's logo or a specific branded product can raise trademark issues even where copyright fair-use might apply. And a meme built around a real, identifiable person's photo or likeness — a celebrity, or worse, a private individual — can raise right-of-publicity claims regardless of the copyright status of the photo itself, especially the moment it's on a product for sale. The safest sellable meme avoids all three categories: no one else's copyrighted image, no logos, no real identifiable faces.
