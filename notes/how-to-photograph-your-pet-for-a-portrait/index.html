How to Photograph Your Pet for a Custom Portrait (Before You Send the Photo)

The single biggest factor in how a custom pet portrait turns out isn't the art style you pick — it's the photo you send. A well-lit, sharp, well-composed photo of a mixed-breed mutt will make a better portrait than a blurry, backlit photo of the most photogenic dog alive, every time, because the artist (human or AI) is working entirely from what's actually in the frame. This is the guide we'd give you before you take that photo, whether it's for a living pet or the one photo you have left of one who isn't.

1. Use natural light, not flash

Overhead flash flattens fur texture, blows out light-colored coats, and creates that red/green eye-shine that's genuinely hard to work with afterward. The best light for a pet photo is open shade or an overcast day outdoors, or a spot near a window indoors with the pet facing the light, not backlit by it. Avoid direct midday sun, which creates harsh shadows across the face. If you only have indoor lighting, turn off the flash and move the pet closer to the brightest window in the house.

2. Get down to eye level

A photo taken from standing height, looking down at your pet, distorts proportions and often loses the eyes to shadow under the brow. Get down on the floor or crouch so the camera is roughly level with your pet's face. This single change does more for a portrait's likeness than any editing afterward — eye-level photos read as "that's them" in a way top-down photos rarely do.

3. Fill the frame, but don't crop the ears or chin

Get close enough that your pet's head and shoulders take up most of the frame — a photo where your pet is a small figure in a big room gives the artist very little detail to work with once it's cropped in. At the same time, leave a little room around the whole head; a photo that already cuts off an ear tip or the top of the head can't be un-cropped later.

4. Check resolution before you send it

A screenshot of a screenshot, or a photo pulled off a social media post (which compresses images heavily), rarely has enough detail for a large or highly detailed portrait style. The original photo file, straight from the camera or phone gallery — not a downloaded, re-shared, or repeatedly-texted copy — is almost always the better source. If you're not sure which version you have, send the largest file size available; you can always downscale, but you can't recover detail that's already been compressed away.

5. Make sure both eyes are visible and in focus

The eyes are where a portrait either does or doesn't capture your pet's actual expression — it's the first thing anyone who knew the pet will look at. A profile shot, a photo where one eye is in shadow, or an action shot where the eyes are motion-blurred all make this much harder. A pet looking roughly toward the camera, both eyes open and lit, is worth more than a dozen candid action shots for portrait purposes — even if the action shots are more fun on their own.

6. What to do if you only have an old or low-quality photo

This comes up most often for memorial portraits, where there's no opportunity to retake the photo. A few things help even with an imperfect source: send the highest-resolution version you can find (check other family members' phones and old cloud backups — a photo texted years ago is often a smaller copy of a still-existing larger original); include a second or third photo of the same pet from a different angle or lighting if you have one, since a second reference can fill in detail the primary photo is missing; and mention any physical detail that's hard to see in the photo but that you remember clearly — a specific eye color, a distinctive marking, a torn ear — so it can be represented even where the source photo doesn't show it well. A blurry, low-light, or old photo is a real constraint on final detail, but it is very rarely a reason not to try; it's the reason to be upfront about it before the work starts, not after.
