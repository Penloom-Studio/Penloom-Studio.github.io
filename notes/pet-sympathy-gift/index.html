What Should I Give Someone Who Lost Their Pet?

You're not the one grieving. You're the friend, the partner, the sister, the colleague — standing just outside it, wanting to do something and worried that the wrong something will make it worse. That worry is a good instinct, and it is also the reason most people end up doing nothing at all. Doing nothing is the one option that reliably hurts.

Here is what tends to help, what tends not to, and how to hand it over.

Is a pet portrait an appropriate sympathy gift?

For most people who were openly attached to their pet, yes — and it is often the gift they keep. What a portrait says, without anyone having to say it aloud, is: your pet was real, they mattered, and they were worth commemorating. Pet loss is a grief people are frequently embarrassed to claim, because the world treats it as a lesser one. A commemorative object quietly refuses that framing, which is why it lands so hard for so many people.

Two situations where it needs more thought. If the loss is very recent and they haven't spoken about it yet, an image of the pet's face may be more than they can hold this week — a note now and the portrait later is kinder than both at once. And if the death was traumatic or the pet was lost rather than died, imagery can carry more weight than you intend; a memorial keepsake without a large portrait of the face is the gentler choice there.

How long should I wait?

Split it in two, and the timing problem mostly solves itself.

  • In the first days: the message, the food, the offer to walk the other dog, the "I'm so sorry about Milo." Immediate, small, no object required.
  • A few weeks later: the memorial gift. This is when the messages stop, everyone else has moved on, and the house is quietest — and it is when a memorial object is most felt.

If you're reading this months afterwards and wondering whether you've missed the window: you haven't. People are frequently more moved by a memorial gift that arrives long after everyone else forgot than by one that arrives in the first week among a hundred others. Late is not too late.

How to give it without making it awkward

  • Privately. Not at a party, not in an office, not in front of a group. Crying in front of an audience is the awkwardness people fear, and it is entirely avoidable.
  • Briefly. Hand it over, say one sentence, stop talking. You are not owed a reaction, and waiting for one turns a gift into a performance.
  • Use the pet's name. "This is for Milo" does more work than any paragraph. People are often desperate to hear the name said out loud by someone else.
  • Give them an exit. "There's no need to open it now" or "put it away if today isn't the day" removes all obligation, and is almost always met with relief.
  • Don't explain the gift. Let the object speak. Talking it up asks them to reassure you that you chose well.

What to write

Four things do nearly all the work: their pet's name, one specific true memory, a plain acknowledgement, and one concrete offer.

I'm so sorry about Bella. I keep thinking about how she used to plant herself at the top of the stairs so nobody could go up without being greeted. Your house is going to feel wrong for a while. I'm dropping dinner round Thursday — you don't have to talk to me when I do.

Things worth avoiding, all of which are said with real kindness and all of which land badly: "at least she had a long life", "it was just a dog", "you can always get another one", "she's in a better place" unless you know that is their belief, and "let me know if you need anything" — which puts the work of asking onto the person with the least capacity to do it. Offer a specific thing instead.

If you're choosing between gifts

Things that tend to be kept: a portrait of the pet; a memorial keepsake with the pet's name and dates; a framed print for a specific spot in the house; a donation in the pet's name to a shelter, with the card that names them. Things that tend not to: anything requiring the grieving person to do a project; anything referencing a future pet; anything with a novelty tone.

If you want to commission a portrait but don't have a photo, a shared album, a group chat, or another family member usually does — you don't need to ask the person you're giving it to. If you do ask them, "do you have a favourite photo of Milo?" is a welcome question far more often than an intrusive one; most people want to talk about their pet and have run out of people willing to let them.
