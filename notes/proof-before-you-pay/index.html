Proof Before You Pay: The Honest Guide to a Pet Memorial Portrait

You are probably reading this in one of two moods. Either your pet is gone and you want something on the wall that looks like them, or you can see the end coming and you want to be ready before you're not. Either way, you are about to hand a photograph — maybe the last good one — to a company you've never met, and trust them to get your pet's face right while you're grieving too hard to argue if they don't.

That trust gets abused more often than it should — usually not through outright fraud, but a smaller betrayal: a seller who lets you believe a human is painting brushstrokes when a machine is generating pixels, and delivers a face that's close enough to pass at a glance and wrong enough to notice every time you walk past it. None of that is inevitable. It happens because buyers don't know what to ask for, and sellers know it. This guide closes that gap — and it's written to be useful whether you buy from us, from someone else, or from no one.

What does "AI portrait" actually mean?

Say the word plainly, because the sellers who don't are the ones worth avoiding. An AI pet portrait is generated by a model trained on enormous numbers of images, given your photo as a reference, and instructed to render it in a chosen style — oil painting, watercolor, pop art, whatever you picked. No brush touches canvas. The output can be genuinely beautiful and genuinely look like your pet, but it is a computed image, not a painted one. This isn't a case against AI portraits — it's a case for calling them what they are. The buyers who end up angriest aren't the ones who chose an AI portrait; they're the ones led to expect a hand-painted one and charged accordingly. Ask directly: "Is this rendered by AI, by a human artist, or some mix of both?" A seller who answers instantly and specifically is telling you the truth is not a liability for them.

Should I have to pay before I see my pet's face?

No — and this is the single most important habit in this guide. You should see a version of YOUR pet's actual face — not a sample, not a similar breed, not a generic template — before you pay the full price. Concretely:

  • The preview is generated from the photo you submitted, not a stock example of "what our portraits look like."
  • You can see it before the final charge is taken, or at minimum before a non-refundable balance is due.
  • If the first attempt doesn't look like your pet, you're told what a revision costs — ideally nothing, up to a stated limit — before you've paid in full.
  • Nobody asks you to pay 100% up front for something you haven't seen any version of. A deposit to begin work is reasonable; a single non-refundable full payment before any preview exists is not.

If a process skips straight from "upload your photo" to "here's your invoice" with no proof step in between, that is the clearest predictor of the wrong-face complaint. Its absence isn't an oversight — it's the step that would let you catch their mistake before you'd paid for it.

How do I tell if the portrait actually looks like my pet?

"It doesn't look like my dog" is the most common complaint in this category, and it's usually true in a specific, checkable way. When you look at a proof, don't ask "is this a nice portrait" — ask whether these survived the translation from photo to art:

  1. Eye shape and color — almond, round, wide- or close-set, and the actual color. The feature owners notice fastest when it's wrong.
  2. Ear set and carriage — upright, folded, one up and one down (which a lot of generated portraits quietly "fix"), cropped or natural.
  3. Muzzle length and shape — where "looks like a generic golden retriever" complaints usually start.
  4. Coat pattern and unique markings — the white chest patch, the one black ear, the freckle on the nose. A template can't have these, so they're the best test of whether the render used your photo.
  5. Proportions — head-to-body ratio, leg length, build.
  6. A characteristic expression, if your photo had one — the head-tilt, the underbite, the ear that never went up.
  7. Age-correct detail — a senior pet's graying muzzle or cloudy eye is part of who they were; a portrait that quietly restores a younger look isn't a memorial of the pet you actually said goodbye to.

If two or more are wrong, you are not looking at your pet with an artistic filter over it — you're looking at a similar-looking template, and you should ask for a real revision before paying anything further. That's not pickiness; it's the difference between a portrait and a coincidence.

What should I ask about delivery before I pay?

Get specific answers before you pay, not after:

  • Digital or physical? A digital file cannot arrive late, damaged, as the wrong pet, or in a box left in the rain. If a physical print matters to you, that's reasonable — but you're also buying into a shipping timeline and everything that can go wrong between a printer and your door.
  • What size, on what material, exactly? "Canvas" and "poster paper in a frame" are not the same product. Ask for the material by name, and whether the frame is included and what it actually is.
  • Does the portrait fit the frame, or the frame fit the portrait? Ask before you order — some portraits arrive sized to a frame you didn't choose, with no way to size up.
  • A real date, not "5–10 business days" with no accountability if it's day 14. Ask what happens if the date is missed.
  • Is there tracking, and will you actually receive the number?

None of this applies to an instant digital download: nothing to ship, arrive damaged, or miss a date. That's not a marketing line — it's just true of digital delivery as a category, worth weighing against how much a physical object matters to you.

What pricing patterns should make me cautious?

  • A subscription for a one-time need. Grief has a shape; it does not renew monthly. Ask what happens if you cancel after the first month, and whether you keep what you made.
  • A "free" app that paywalls the output. Free to download and preview, then a wall the moment you try to save, export, or remove a watermark. If the free version can't produce a finished thing you keep, it's a very long advertisement.
  • A price that changes between the page and the invoice — "add-ons," rush fees, "premium" style unlocks sprung mid-checkout. A fair seller prices the whole thing up front, once.
  • No stated revision or refund policy at all. Not necessarily a generous one — just a stated one. Its absence usually means you're the one who'll absorb the cost when it goes wrong.

What makes a memorial piece worth keeping?

If you'll live with this image for years, a few practical things matter more than the style you picked:

  • Resolution. For anything printed, you want a source file well above screen resolution — roughly 300 DPI at your intended print size. Ask what resolution you're receiving, in pixels, not just "high quality."
  • A backup you control. You should receive the actual file, not just access to view it inside someone's app. Apps close; accounts get deactivated; a file in your own downloads folder outlasts any company.
  • Clear usage rights — you own the right to print, reprint, and share the portrait of your own pet.
  • A style that will still feel right in five years. Classic renderings — oil, watercolor, simple line art — tend to age better on a wall than trend-driven looks.

The checklist

Screenshot this and use it with any seller — including us. If you can check every box before you pay, you are in good hands, whoever you choose.

  • I've been told plainly whether this is AI-generated, human-painted, or both.
  • I will see a preview made from MY pet's actual photo before paying the full price.
  • I know what a revision costs if the first version doesn't look right.
  • I know the exact material and size of what I'll receive — not a general category.
  • I know whether the portrait is sized to a frame or the frame is sized to the portrait.
  • I have a real delivery date, and I know what happens if it's missed.
  • There's a stated refund or revision policy, in writing, before I pay.
  • I'm not being asked to subscribe to something I only need once.
  • I know the resolution of the file I'm actually receiving.
  • I will own a copy of the file itself, not just access to view it somewhere.

None of this is about being suspicious of everyone who does this work — most people in it lost a pet themselves and wanted to build something better than what they found. It's about not having to find that out the hard way, at the one moment you have the least capacity to fight for yourself. You already know what your pet looked like. You shouldn't have to argue with anyone about it.
