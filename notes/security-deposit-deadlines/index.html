How Long Does a Landlord Have to Return My Security Deposit?

Verified states. The table below covers five states whose statutes we read and checked ourselves. Each row carries its citation and the date it was verified. This is not a national rule and it is not a fifty-state table.

Your lease does not set this deadline. Your state does, the clock usually starts when the tenancy ends or the unit is surrendered, and in most states the landlord owes you a written itemization of every deduction — not just the leftover money.

This is general news and education, not legal advice. It describes the statutes named below as they were written and verified on the dates stamped in each row. It cannot know your facts and creates no attorney-client relationship. State law varies enormously and deadlines for bringing a claim are short — before a filing, a signature, or a deadline, consult a licensed attorney in your state.

Do Different States Have Different Security Deposit Laws?

Yes, and the spread is wide: fourteen days in New York against forty-five days for the balance in Illinois, with entirely different penalty structures behind them. There is no national deadline to fall back on.

Two currency points, because older guides get both wrong

  • Illinois now covers everyone. The Security Deposit Return Act used to apply only to larger buildings. Public Act 103-224, effective January 1, 2024, struck that threshold: the statute now reads simply "a lessor of residential real property," with no minimum unit count anywhere in the current text. Any guide still printing a five-unit minimum is telling Illinois tenants renting a house or a two-flat that they have no remedy when they do.
  • California now expects photographs. AB 2801, effective April 1, 2025, added photo-documentation requirements around damage claims to Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5.

How Long Can a Landlord Hold My Security Deposit After I Move Out?

Only as long as the row above allows, and the clock is tied to a defined event — vacating, surrender, or the end of the tenancy, depending on the state — not to when the landlord gets round to inspecting. Two practical steps protect the count: give a forwarding address in writing and note the date you did it, and keep proof of when you handed back the keys. Where a state permits a claim instead of a return, as Florida does within thirty days, the landlord still has to act inside the window; missing it is the violation.

What Happens If My Landlord Doesn't Provide an Itemized List of Charges?

In several states, that omission is the violation — independent of whether the deductions would have been justified. New York is the sharpest example: missing the 14-day deadline forfeits the right to retain any part of the deposit (N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108). This is why silence from a landlord is frequently a stronger position for a tenant than a long itemized list, and why "they never sent anything" is worth writing down with the date you expected it.

What Counts as 'Normal Wear and Tear' in an Apartment?

No federal law defines the phrase, and no state hands you a list of scuffs. What statutes do instead is define the deductible categories, leaving ordinary deterioration outside them. California's structure at Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5(b) is the clearest illustration: deductions are permitted for unpaid rent, for repair of damages caused by the tenant exceeding ordinary wear and tear, and for cleaning to return the unit to the level of cleanliness it had at the inception of the tenancy.

Read that last clause carefully, because it is where cleaning and carpet charges live or die: the benchmark is the condition at move-in, which is a factual question answered by evidence, not by argument. A charge to make the unit cleaner than you received it is a different thing from a charge to restore it. That is a matter of state law and of proof, and it is the single most common line item disputed in small claims.

What Should I Document Before Moving Out to Protect My Deposit?

The record is the case. Assemble it before the keys change hands:

  • Dated photographs of every room, floor, appliance and wall — taken at move-in and again at move-out, from the same positions.
  • The signed lease and any move-in condition checklist, however informal.
  • Proof you paid the deposit: the cancelled cheque, the bank record, the receipt, or the lease clause acknowledging it.
  • The date and method by which you gave your forwarding address, and the date you surrendered the unit.
  • Every written exchange about the condition of the unit, kept in full rather than screenshotted.

California put part of this into statute — AB 2801, effective April 1, 2025, added photo-documentation requirements around damage claims to § 1950.5 — but the photographs are the cheapest evidence available to a tenant in any state, verified or not.

How Do I File a Complaint About My Landlord's Deposit Practices?

Two routes, and they are not alternatives — many tenants use both.

  • Your state attorney general's consumer-protection office, and in many states a housing or landlord-tenant agency at state or city level.
  • Small claims court, which is how most deposit statutes are actually enforced. The penalty multipliers above exist precisely because the tenant is expected to be the enforcer.

Before either, one letter usually does more than a phone call: name the address and the dates, name the statute and its deadline, ask for one specific amount, give a date fourteen days out, and state plainly what you will do next. A letter you can prove you sent — certified mail, return receipt kept with your copy — is worth several you cannot.

What this page does not tell you

It does not cover the other forty-five states, and it does not state your deadline for bringing a claim, which every state sets separately and which this page does not attempt. A perfect demand letter sent after that deadline is a piece of paper. If real money or your housing is at stake, find out your state's limit first — and if you have been served with anything, talk to a licensed attorney rather than a table.
