The midwife of Nagyrév

Hungary, 1911–1929

An eighteen-year poisoning economy in interwar Hungary — and the famous victim count that the evidence does not support.

In the Tiszazug, a poor bend of the Tisza river, a midwife named Zsuzsanna Fazekas arrived around 1911 and began boiling arsenic out of flypaper and selling it. For roughly eighteen years, women in Nagyrév and the villages around it used it on husbands, fathers-in-law, parents, and in some cases children.

Why it worked for eighteen years

It worked because Fazekas was the only medical authority for miles: the deaths went down as cholera, and nobody audited her. It ended in 1929 with an anonymous letter to a regional newspaper. Investigators exhumed the cemetery; dozens of bodies carried arsenic. Thirty-four women and one man were arrested. Fazekas poisoned herself first.

The number you were told is wrong

Debunked — you will read that there were three hundred victims. That figure comes from the sensational press of 1929, not from evidence. Béla Bodó, who worked through the court files, puts the credible count at around forty-five to fifty. That is still an eighteen-year poisoning economy inside interwar Europe. It does not need inflating.
