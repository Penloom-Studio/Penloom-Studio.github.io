The free attraction behind Notre-Dame

Paris, 1864–1907

For forty-three years the Paris morgue was open to the public, daily, free, and it was in the guidebooks.

From 1864 the Paris morgue stood on the quay directly behind Notre-Dame, and its exhibition hall was open to the public every day from dawn until six, free of charge. Unidentified dead — many taken from the Seine — lay on marble slabs behind plate glass, their clothes hung beside them so a visitor might recognise a coat.

What it became

The stated purpose was identification. What it became was entertainment. It was in the guidebooks; Dickens wrote about going; Zola staged a scene there. When a sensational body came in — the unidentified child of the rue du Vert-Bois in 1886 is the famous case — the queue stopped traffic on the bridge. Historians argue it was one of the first true mass entertainments.

On the crowd numbers

Disputed — the papers reported tens of thousands of visitors in a day. Treat those as press claims, not counted attendance; nobody worked a turnstile. The building, the glass, the free daily opening and the closure are documented. The prefect of police shut the hall on 15 March 1907, over the objections of the reporters who worked the corpse beat.
