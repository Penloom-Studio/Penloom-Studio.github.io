The sourcing standard

Why Do Some Historical Facts Sound Like Urban Legends?

Two independent reputable sources, minimum — or it does not get published. Where the record is contested, we print DISPUTED and name the contested part. That rule is the whole business.

Because in most of the strange stories you have been told, the strangest part is the invented part. Not the whole story — the detail. The number. The dying quote. The motive. Strip that one element away and what is left is usually still true, still documented, and still worth telling. The reason so much real history feels fake is that the version that reached you was optimised for retelling, not for accuracy, and every retelling sanded one more caveat off it.

There are three different things happening under that one feeling, and it is worth being able to tell them apart:

  1. It is true and it was documented as it happened. A city government hiring musicians to treat an outbreak of compulsive dancing sounds like folklore. It is in the municipal record. Reality does not owe you plausibility.
  2. It is true with a lie welded on. The event is real; the quotable detail was added later by someone who needed a better ending. This is the most common case by a wide margin, and it is the one that gets people burned at dinner, because the fact-check comes back "half right."
  3. It is folklore wearing a record's clothes. Repeated confidently for long enough, with each retelling citing the last, until it has the texture of documentation and none of the substance.

The tell for all three is the same, and you can apply it in about ninety seconds: the single most quotable detail is the one to check first. If the story survives losing it, the story is probably real. If the story only exists because of it, be careful.

The standard everything here is held to

Nothing is published under this name unless it traces to at least two independent reputable sources. Reputable means one of four things:

  • a peer-reviewed paper;
  • a national archive or equivalent institutional collection;
  • a court, coroner or government record — hearings, filings, inquest findings;
  • a book by a named historian working from the above.

Popular-history sites, encyclopaedia entries and magazine features are used only to corroborate a claim that already rests on one of those four. They never carry a claim on their own. Independent means the two sources do not both descend from the same original — two articles quoting the same 1970s paperback are one source wearing two coats.

And where the record is genuinely contested, the story says Disputed and names which part is contested. Not a disclaimer at the bottom. A flag on the sentence.

Three worked examples

1518

The dancing plague of Strasbourg

What survives checking: people danced compulsively in the street for weeks; the authorities concluded the cure was more dancing and cleared guildhalls and hired musicians. This sits in municipal records, physicians' notes and cathedral sermons of the period.

Disputed The cause and the death toll. Ergot poisoning is the popular explanation and is medically weak — ergotism causes convulsions and gangrene, not sustained coordinated dancing. The "fifteen people a day died" line traces to a single much later chronicle and is not supported by the 1518 sources. Which is exactly the pattern: the most repeatable number is the least supported one.

1932

The "Emu War"

What survives checking: Australian soldiers were deployed to Western Australia with two Lewis guns and ten thousand rounds against emus damaging wheat crops; the birds scattered, a gun jammed early, and the operation was withdrawn without meeting its objective. That is in the operation's own records and in the contemporary press.

Disputed The kill count. The much-quoted "986 killed" is the commanding officer's own end-of-operation claim, never independently verified, and contemporary reports conflict. It is a claimed figure, not a counted one — and every viral version prints it as a counted one.

1938

The War of the Worlds panic

What survives checking: the broadcast happened, and some listeners were genuinely frightened — the surviving letters prove it.

Disputed The nationwide mass panic. Audience measurement from the night shows a comparatively small live audience, and the scholarship argues the panic was substantially a newspaper construction — the press had a commercial interest in portraying radio as dangerous. This is the rare case where the famous version is the invented one and the boring version is the record.

How to check one yourself, in four moves

  1. Isolate the quotable detail. The number, the last words, the motive. That is where the invention lives.
  2. Follow the chain down until you hit a document. Article citing article citing article is not a chain, it is an echo. Stop when you reach a filing, a hearing, an archive item or a historian's footnote.
  3. Ask whether the number was counted or estimated. Both are legitimate; conflating them is not. "476 counted at a 1936 hearing" and "roughly 764 by later epidemiological reconstruction" are different claims about the same disaster, and both are true.
  4. Date the earliest source that carries the wild part. If the wildest element first appears a century after the event, someone added it.

That is the entire method. It is not sophisticated. It is just rarely done, which is why so much confidently delivered history is wrong in one specific place.
